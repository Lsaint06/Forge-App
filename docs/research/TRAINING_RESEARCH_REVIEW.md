# Forge training research review — first pass

Research date: October 14, 2026 (America/Los_Angeles).

These are repository research notes, added with the user’s permission. No application code, saved programs or workout history were changed. This document is not an approved implementation specification. Further investigation is recorded in CONTINUING_RESEARCH.md.

## Scope and method

Investigated the five requested figures: Arnold Schwarzenegger, Ronnie Coleman, Jay Cutler, Rich Piana and Chris Bumstead. Added Dorian Yates, Frank Zane, Hany Rambod's FST-7 material, Renaissance Periodization, Greg Nuckols/Stronger by Science and Menno Henselmans to examine competing approaches. At the time of the initial pass, Jeff Nippard's selected article pages returned 404. This gap was subsequently resolved through his official public Atom feed; see [Continuing research](CONTINUING_RESEARCH.md).

This is not an exhaustive review of every Olympian, influencer, book, video or article. It is a documented initial review of accessible material relevant to program design. Athlete methods can change by year, coach, competition phase and recovery circumstances. A single published routine does not establish a person's lifelong practice or advice for a novice.

The review distinguishes:

- Attributed athlete/coaching articles: evidence of the author's stated approach, not controlled evidence of universal effectiveness. An athlete byline can still involve editorial assistance.
- Official brand/team material: evidence of what the organization publishes; not necessarily the athlete's own words. Commercial incentives remain relevant.
- Secondary routine reports: leads and examples; weaker evidence of what someone actually did.
- Research full text: relevant methods, results, recommendations and limitations inspected.
- Research abstracts: conclusions limited to the abstract; no claim to have assessed inaccessible methods, tables or supplementary material.

Search-result snippets, product descriptions and successfully loaded homepages were not treated as reviewed training evidence. A legacy Bodybuilding.com Blueprint URL returned a storefront rather than the original program. YouTube access failed; no videos or video transcripts were reviewed. Some search requests encountered bot challenges; those challenges were not bypassed.

## Findings by person or organization

### Arnold Schwarzenegger

Sources A1–A3 below. Arnold's Pump Club is a team publication, with Arnold listed as publisher and Adam Bornstein and Daniel Ketchell as editors. Its rest article discusses three working sets per exercise and longer rest; its habits/volume article prioritizes consistency, effort and total volume. These are current general-audience messages, not direct documentation of his competition-era workouts.

Muscle & Strength presents historical routines attributed to Arnold and references The New Encyclopedia of Modern Bodybuilding. The page describes high-volume/high-frequency splits and lists multiple exercises with 3–4 sets. This is a secondary representation; the original book was not read or independently checked in this session.

Useful implications: complementary exercises, repeated muscle exposure, deliberate effort and consistent training. Do not automatically copy six-day, high-volume routines to everyone. Do not equate the current newsletter with a historical competition routine.

### Ronnie Coleman

Sources R1–R2. An M&F article attributed to Ronnie emphasizes challenging loading, 10–15 repetitions, controlled full range of motion, pumps and supersets. It also says he does not go to failure in that described approach. This matters because internet summaries often collapse all Ronnie training into maximal loads and absolute failure.

Muscle & Brawn supplies a secondary six-day routine, including many exercises with three sets. Its attached direct interview principally concerns a possible comeback and time off; that interview does not independently authenticate every set in the routine table.

Useful implications: train with purpose, develop complementary movements, use meaningful resistance relative to the person, and preserve progression. Mechanistic claims in the athlete article about striations, blood volume and muscle fibers should not become established scientific explanations in Forge. The article is one attributed account, not proof that Ronnie always trained exactly this way.

### Jay Cutler

Sources J1–J2. M&F's historical high-volume leg article quotes Jay identifying as a volume trainer. Its example contains many exercises and three- or four-set prescriptions. It emphasizes diverse exercise coverage and enough recovery between demanding sessions.

The official Cutler Club store lists a high-volume guide, but only the product page was accessible/reviewed, not the paid guide. No claim is made to have read that book.

Useful implications: bodybuilding needs sufficiently developed exercise coverage, including complementary quadriceps and hamstring work. High volume must be supported by recovery. Do not transfer an example professional leg session directly to the default program of an ordinary user. The article's recovery interval is an athlete/coaching recommendation, not a universal biological rule.

### Rich Piana

Source P1. His brand's retrospective describes feeder workouts as light, high-repetition sessions for a previously trained muscle. It gives examples of 30–50 or 100 repetitions and attributes the method to a belief in frequently pumping or “feeding” the muscle. It also describes moderate loads, exercise variety, pyramids and controlled repetitions.

This is a brand account after his lifetime, not a direct interview with him and not controlled evidence for feeder sessions. Its supplement recommendations are promotional.

Useful implications: lighter absolute loads can still be challenging; execution and target-muscle work matter. The reviewed research does not establish a special feeder-workout mechanism or justify automatically adding nightly 100-repetition sessions. Do not treat a pump as proof of long-term growth or add speculative recovery work without counting its fatigue.

### Chris Bumstead (CBum)

Source C1 is a secondary Generation Iron routine report. It lists many exercises with two or three sets, an eight-day cycle, complementary angles, machines and isolation movements. However, the same page mixes competition-era language with post-retirement language, and other search results claim different split lengths. Those conflicts reduce confidence in presenting it as his exact current routine.

Original embedded video links were identified, but YouTube access was denied. No original CBum video, transcript, complete log or personal program was reviewed. A precise CBum set count, split or progression rule remains unverified.

Useful implication: avoid substituting celebrity attribution for reliable program design. The secondary example illustrates that two or three sets per exercise is compatible with a larger bodybuilding session; it does not prove an optimal dose or authenticate his current plan. More primary material is needed before Forge labels anything CBum-inspired.

### Dorian Yates and Mike Mentzer's influence

Source D1 is an M&F article attributed to Yates. He describes moving from more sets to two, then to one main working set per exercise after years of experience, with substantial warm-ups and exceptional effort. He describes Mentzer's influence; Mentzer's own writings were not independently reviewed here.

Useful implications: distinguish warm-ups from working sets and account for effort and training history. The article directly contradicts the proposition that every successful Olympian always used more than three working sets per exercise. It does not prove one set is optimal for everyone, nor justify forced repetitions or beyond-failure methods as novice defaults.

### Frank Zane

Source Z1 is an article on his official site explaining tri-sets, exercise pairings and substantial rest between demanding tri-sets. It discusses reducing interference between successive movements.

Useful implications: exercise order, local fatigue and session efficiency matter. Its claims about clearing toxins or healing soreness/injuries via blood flow are not established by the evidence reviewed and should not become app guidance. Expertise in building a physique does not make every proposed mechanism reliable.

### Hany Rambod / Evogen FST-7

Source H1 describes the seven-set finishing component of FST-7 and emphasizes controlled execution, target-muscle selection, diet and recovery. It also makes strong mechanistic claims involving fascia, metabolites and anabolic hormones.

Useful implications: finishers are a component of a larger program, not a requirement for every workout. The seven-set format is not established as universally superior by the reviewed evidence. Acute hormonal or pump explanations do not establish greater long-term hypertrophy. This is coach-associated brand material, not proof that every Rambod client follows one identical plan.

### Renaissance Periodization / Mike Israetel-associated framework

Source K1 explains maintenance, minimum effective, maximum adaptive and maximum recoverable volume. It defines assumptions about load, repetitions and proximity to failure and uses a direct-set counting convention with indirect work already considered in its recommendations.

Useful implications: starting volume, response and recoverability should be distinguished. Its volume landmarks are a coaching framework, not precisely measurable fixed thresholds for every individual. Do not combine its direct-set recommendations with another paper's fractional-set model without reconciling the counting convention. Its assertion that different rep ranges give similar fatigue should not be applied universally.

### Greg Nuckols / Stronger by Science

Sources K2–K3 discuss the breadth of hypertrophy-effective repetition ranges, practical efficiency, strength specificity, training age and individual circumstances.

Useful implications: moderate repetition targets can be practical without being the only effective range. Strength programming requires more than hypertrophy with lower reps. These coaching syntheses are useful interpretation, but newer research should take precedence where it materially updates an older conclusion.

### Menno Henselmans

Source K4 discusses frequency and recovery in relation to workload, training experience and life stress. Its precise recovery timings and sex-specific adjustments are coaching interpretations and not universal scheduling rules. No fixed hours-to-recovery table should be adopted without further appraisal.

## Research synthesis

### 1. Volume matters, with diminishing returns

S1 (2021 IUSCA, full-text relevant sections) recommends approximately ten sets per muscle weekly as a general hypertrophy-optimization starting benchmark, while acknowledging meaningful responses at lower volumes and potential benefit from more. It recommends spreading higher volumes across sessions and discusses specialization by reallocating work.

S2 (Pelland et al., 2026 issue, DOI assigned 2025; abstract reviewed) uses 67 studies and distinguishes direct and indirect work. More weekly volume was associated with larger gains, with diminishing returns. Fractional counting of indirect sets fit their models better than counting every indirect set fully or ignoring it entirely. This supports accounting for compound assistance; it does not make “every indirect set equals half a set” an exact physiological law for every muscle/exercise.

S3 (2026 ACSM, relevant full-text sections) synthesizes 137 reviews. Higher volume was associated with hypertrophy, but the authors explicitly say the exact number needed to optimize adaptations cannot be ascertained. Much of the underlying evidence concerns inexperienced participants. Its broad adult guidance is not a validated advanced bodybuilding algorithm.

Design implication: audit weekly muscle coverage and prescribe a reasoned starting dose, but do not hard-code ten direct sets as a universal minimum or twenty as a universal maximum. The counting method and population must be explicit. Do not assume adding sets everywhere improves the plan.

### 2. Relative effort matters more than comparing users' kilograms

S4 (2017 low-/high-load meta-analysis, abstract) found similar hypertrophy across loading ranges under the studied conditions, which included training sets to momentary failure. Higher loads were more effective for 1RM strength. Those conditions matter: light weights stopped far from fatigue should not be assumed equivalent.

S5 (2024 proximity-to-failure meta-regressions, abstract) found closer-to-failure training associated with greater hypertrophy, whereas the strength relationship differed. RIR was estimated and the analysis exploratory; the exact optimal RIR remains uncertain. S3 also cautions that exact RIR targets cannot be established from current evidence.

Design implication: lighter lifters deserve equally rigorous programming and explanation. Provide effort guidance and calibration; do not derive training quality from absolute load. A proposed 1–3 RIR range is a practical coaching starting point, not a proven universal optimum.

### 3. Two or three working sets is not evidence of a scam

Individual exercise sets must be interpreted alongside the number of exercises, frequency and effort. Some reviewed elite-associated examples use 2–4 sets per exercise; Yates's account uses fewer main sets with very different execution and context.

For example, two chest exercises × three working sets × two weekly sessions gives twelve direct chest sets per week. One chest exercise × three sets × one session gives three. Both can display “3 sets.” Neither the number alone nor a celebrity comparison determines whether the individual program is suitable.

S3 notes strength benefits from two to three sets in its broad evidence synthesis and emphasizes diminishing returns. A blanket increase to four or five sets on every lift would not be evidence-based.

### 4. Rest recommendations need nuance

S6 (2024 rest meta-analysis, relevant full-text/abstract) found a small tendency favoring rests longer than 60 seconds, with substantial overlap and uncertainty. It did not detect appreciable hypertrophy differences beyond 90 seconds in its dataset. This is more nuanced than “three minutes always builds more muscle.” S1 recommends longer rests for demanding multi-joint work and allows shorter rests for appropriate isolation exercises.

Design implication: rest should support the next quality set and reflect exercise, loading and effort. Longer rests can be appropriate, especially for demanding compounds or strength practice, without being a mandatory three-minute rule for every exercise. Do not shorten rest silently to fit a clock.

### 5. Frequency and split are organizational tools

S7 (2019 frequency review, abstract) and S8 (2024 split/full-body meta-analysis, abstract) found similar hypertrophy outcomes when volume was equated. S2 suggests frequency's relationship with strength differs from its relationship with hypertrophy.

Design implication: select full body, upper/lower or another split around availability, recoverable session size, preferences and lift practice. Six days is not automatically better than three; a novice is not necessarily required to train at most three days if workload is suitably distributed. Avoid turning a useful default into a universal prohibition.

### 6. Advanced methods are optional tools

S9 (2026 review, abstract) found no clear general advantage for advanced resistance-training methods over traditional approaches in untrained to moderately trained people; some performance outcomes and contexts differed. Elite lifters remain less well represented.

Design implication: drop sets, rest-pause, giant sets and FST-7-style finishers may be useful preferences or efficiency options. They are not mandatory ingredients or evidence of a more effective plan merely because the session feels harder. Full range of motion and controlled execution are sensible defaults; do not promote one fashionable range-of-motion technique as universally superior without exercise-specific appraisal.

### 7. Body recomposition needs nutrition/recovery context

S10 (energy-deficit meta-analysis, abstract) found energy deficiency impaired lean-mass gains while strength gains were not comparably impaired. Its estimated deficit threshold is a group-level model, not an individual's exact cutoff.

S11 (protein meta-analysis, abstract) found protein supplementation could augment training adaptations, with the modeled breakpoint around 1.6 g/kg/day in the studied healthy adults. This is not a universal personal ceiling or evidence a branded supplement is required.

Design implication: LEAN & STRONG should retain challenging resistance training and account for recovery. It should not imply short rests, high repetitions, sweating or a pump uniquely cause fat loss. Nutrition guidance is separate from inventing a lifting routine that guarantees recomposition.

### 8. Athletic performance requires its own definition

S3 distinguishes power-oriented loading/execution from strength and hypertrophy. S12 (2022 concurrent-training review, relevant full text/abstract) found aerobic plus resistance training generally compatible with hypertrophy and maximal strength, while explosive strength could be attenuated, particularly with same-session training.

Design implication: ATHLETE MODE needs a defined objective and context, including sport practice, conditioning and power demands. A generic controlled-lifting block can be useful but must be labeled as such. Adding random jumps or reducing reps does not establish an optimized athletic program.

## Recommendations for Forge — research conclusions, not implementation authorization

1. Use goal-specific success criteria rather than judging all six Paths by bodybuilding volume.
2. Distinguish absolute load, relative load and effort. Lighter loads never imply that a user deserves a less carefully designed plan.
3. Define a working set and show warm-ups separately.
4. Audit individual muscles and movement coverage, including distinctions obscured by the current broad “pull,” “posterior” and “shoulder” categories.
5. Separate direct and indirect work; clearly label any equivalence model as an estimate.
6. Provide set/rep/rest/effort prescriptions with a rationale. Never claim an exact universal optimum.
7. Use appropriate starting workloads and subsequent real performance/recovery information; a questionnaire alone cannot discover a person's optimal recoverable dose.
8. Explain duration/equipment tradeoffs rather than silently cutting substantive work.
9. Use specialization by reallocating workload, not automatically increasing every muscle's volume.
10. Preserve completed workout snapshots, scheduling links and both four-week phase references if changes are later authorized. No rotation engine is implied by this research.
11. Do not label a program athlete-inspired without an attributable original source and a clear account of the adaptation.
12. Define representative generated-program review cases and quality checks before implementation. A successful software test does not validate real-world muscle growth.

The current code audit already established that Forge uses coarse movement roles, uniform starting set rules and automatic set reduction to meet duration. Those are confirmed software behaviors. The best replacement rules still need a reviewed specification. This document does not establish that any generated routine is “optimal for everyone.”

## Outstanding evidence gaps

- Original CBum videos/transcripts or dated logs; primary confirmation of the particular training block under discussion.
- Complete original books/guides from Arnold, Jay and Mentzer; these were not read in full.
- Initial Jeff Nippard access gap: subsequently resolved via the official public feed; see CONTINUING_RESEARCH.md for reviewed entries.
- No comprehensive coverage of all Olympia divisions, winners, eras or influencers.
- No direct outcomes study testing Forge-generated programs.
- No established way to infer a person's uniquely optimal dose from their initial questionnaire.

The notes are a reusable project reference, not permanent model training or a claim of exhaustive knowledge. Future implementation should cite this review and resolve the relevant gaps rather than rely on conversational memory.
