This supplements [the initial review](TRAINING_RESEARCH_REVIEW.md). Source identifiers below use B/C prefixes to avoid confusing them with the initial register. ## Update 1 — Jeff Nippard primary-publication gap resolved

The normal article pages returned 404, but the official public Atom feed contains the article bodies: https://jeffnippard.com/blogs/news.atom . This is a legitimate publicly published feed, not a video transcript or access to a paid program. Its archived entries have explicit publication dates. Article bodies were retrieved from that feed; the corresponding page URLs may still fail. No claim is made to have watched the linked videos.

### B1 — “How Hard Should You Train to Build Muscle?” (May 13, 2024)

https://jeffnippard.com/blogs/news/how-hard-should-you-train-to-build-muscle

Article body reviewed from official feed. Nippard recommends sufficiently challenging work while distinguishing failure from close-to-failure training. His practical recommendations differ by experience and exercise, with greater caution on technically demanding compounds. He also discusses experiencing failure on appropriate exercises to calibrate RIR.

Assessment: useful coaching guidance, consistent with the broad distinction between effort and absolute load. The exact proposed RIR ranges are coaching prescriptions, not a proven optimum for every trainee or exercise. A beginner's inability to estimate RIR accurately should not be ignored. Do not turn “failure calibration” into unsupervised maximal attempts on squats or deadlifts.

### B2 — “How many sets do you need?” (April 29, 2024)

https://jeffnippard.com/blogs/news/how-many-sets-do-you-need

Article body reviewed from official feed. Discusses training history, time availability, specialization and differing responses of different muscles. Offers progressively higher starting-volume ranges with training experience and suggests testing higher volume selectively instead of increasing everything.

Important counting difference: this article counts a compound set fully toward multiple involved muscles. RP's reviewed recommendations use a direct-work convention with indirect contributions already considered. Pelland et al.'s newer research compared counting methods and favored fractional accounting in its models. The ranges from these sources cannot be compared or blended without adjusting their definitions.

Implication: Forge must document its accounting method before selecting target ranges. Ten “sets” can describe materially different workloads. A questionnaire should distinguish previous training exposure from how much someone currently lifts.

### B3 — “Junk Volume: Why You Must Avoid It For Max Muscle” (September 4, 2023)

https://jeffnippard.com/blogs/news/junk-volume-why-you-must-avoid-it-for-max-muscle

Article body reviewed from official feed. Separates excessive per-session work, easy sets and very-high-repetition work. Recommends distributing large workloads rather than accumulating all work in one session; explicitly acknowledges inter-person and inter-muscle differences.

Important qualification: its approximate six-set-per-muscle/session discussion draws partly on an in-house analysis and coaching judgment. The author includes exceptions and qualifications. It should not become a hard rule that the seventh set is useless. “Junk volume” is not a validated binary label determined from set count alone. The newer volume research does not establish that exact universal cutoff.

Implication: record session distribution, effort and recovery. Avoid both automatic volume inflation and a rigid universal cap derived from an influencer summary.

## Update 2 — Natural-bodybuilding coaching, time constraints and flexible schedules

### B4 — Brian Minor / 3DMJ, “Time Saving Training Strategies” (January 23, 2025)

https://3dmusclejourney.com/blog/time-saving-training-strategies

Full article text reviewed. Discusses exercise selection/execution first, antagonistic and unrelated-muscle paired sets, machine convenience, extended-set methods, and flexible scheduling. The author distinguishes his own experience from study results. He also cautions that acute EMG differences do not validate long-term hypertrophy claims.

Useful distinctions:

- Alternating opposing muscles is not the same intervention as pre-exhausting one muscle with consecutive exercises.
- Time savings and equivalent adaptations do not establish superior growth.
- A set plus several drops cannot automatically be counted as the same number of rested straight sets.
- Real gyms may not allow convenient simultaneous access to multiple stations.
- A disrupted calendar is not a reason to discard the training block or erase progress.

Implication: potential future efficiency options must be explicit and tested. Do not silently shorten all rests or substitute advanced methods. The user's requested pause/resume/reschedule behavior aligns with the principle of continuing useful training despite imperfect weeks, but this coaching article does not establish one algorithm for recovering missed sessions.

### B5 — Jeff Alberts / 3DMJ, “How to Succeed in Any Training Environment” (page dated April 15, 2025)

https://3dmusclejourney.com/blog/how-to-succeed-in-any-training-environment

Full article text reviewed. Discusses adapting consistent, deliberate practice to home equipment and reports his personal home-training experience. The page contains pandemic-era wording despite its displayed date, so the page date should not be treated as proof of original publication date. It is motivational/coaching material, not a controlled comparison of home and commercial-gym programs.

Implication: limited equipment should trigger thoughtful adaptation, not a presumption that the user deserves an inferior routine. It cannot guarantee that every equipment/exclusion combination supports every goal.

## Update 3 — Exercise selection and specificity

### C1 — Kassiano et al. (2022), exercise variation systematic review

https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000004258

Abstract reviewed. Eight studies, 241 participants, all young men. Systematic variation may benefit regional development and dynamic strength; excessive random variation or redundant selections may impair adaptation. The evidence does not establish an automatic week-five replacement rule.

Implication: complementary exercise selection matters more than novelty. Keeping selections consistent through the approved eight-week Phase A can support practice and measurement; future rotation should have a clear purpose rather than happen because an arbitrary date arrived.

### C2 — Haugen et al. (2023), free-weight versus machine meta-analysis

https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-023-00713-4

Abstract reviewed. No detected hypertrophy advantage for either equipment type in the included studies. Strength changes depended partly on how strength was tested, supporting task specificity. Only a subset of included studies measured hypertrophy; absence of a detected difference is not proof that every exercise pair is interchangeable.

Implication: machine training is not a lesser tier. For a strength goal tied to a particular barbell lift, practice of that task remains relevant. For hypertrophy, choose appropriate exercises based on coverage, comfort, available equipment, execution and progression.

### C3 — Nunes et al. (2021), exercise-order meta-analysis

https://doi.org/10.1080/17461391.2020.1733672

Abstract reviewed. Strength gains tended to favor exercises performed earlier. No significant overall hypertrophy difference was detected between the compared multi-joint/single-joint orders.

Implication: put the highest-priority performance task early, especially for strength or power. “Compounds always first” is a useful default in some contexts, not an inviolable hypertrophy law.

## Update 4 — Progression and individual response

### C4 — “Effects of Resistance Training Overload Progression Protocols on Strength and Muscle Mass” (2024)

https://doi.org/10.1055/a-2256-5857

Abstract reviewed. Within-participant trial of 39 previously untrained young men/women. Increasing repetitions and increasing load both produced improvements with no detected between-protocol difference for the reported outcomes.

Implication: adding weight every session is not the only legitimate progression method. Rep progression can matter when available load increments are coarse. The trial does not establish equivalence in all advanced lifters or all exercises.

### C5 — “Progressive Overload Affects the Magnitude of Muscle Hypertrophy” (2026)

https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000003968

Abstract reviewed. Untrained young women performed elbow extensions; progressive loading produced greater growth than keeping load and repetitions unchanged. The nonprogressive condition also grew over the short study.

Implication: novice gains do not excuse omitting a progression rationale. However, fixed loads can still produce initial gains, and every session need not set a personal record. Avoid generalizing one muscle/exercise trial into a complete universal progression engine.

### C6 — Individual response and prior volume

- “Muscle Hypertrophy Responses to Changes in Training Volume: A Retrospective Analysis” (2023), abstract reviewed: https://doi.org/10.1055/a-2053-8426 . Associations between changes from habitual volume and hypertrophy were weak to moderate. Observational retrospective analysis cannot prove a required personal increase.
- “Is there a volume saturation point for muscle hypertrophy in resistance-trained individuals?” (2025), narrative-review abstract reviewed: https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-025-05959-z . Discusses methodological limitations, prior workload and heterogeneous findings. A narrative review is not a new pooled causal estimate.
- “The Effect of Resistance Training Volume on Individual-Level Skeletal Muscle Adaptations” (2025), PREPRINT abstract reviewed: https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.07.24.666533 . A small repeated unilateral design emphasizes that apparent individual variability can reflect measurement error and within-person variation. It found a group-level hypertrophy advantage for higher volume but could not establish irrefutable meaningful differential individual response. Treat as provisional, not peer-reviewed consensus.

Implication: take prior workload and repeated trends seriously, but do not infer a person's “genetic responder type” from a few sessions or a noisy tape measurement. Personalization needs consistent measurements and honest uncertainty.

### C7 — Large volume increase trial (2026)

https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00284.2026

Abstract reviewed. In 25 trained men/women using a unilateral leg design, increasing habitual volume by 120% did not outperform a 20% increase for measured hypertrophy; the larger increase did not show impaired adaptation under the study conditions either.

Implication: neither “huge jumps are always harmful” nor “more must be better” follows. This small exercise-specific trial cannot determine every user's workload ceiling.

## Update 5 — Periodization, deloads and pauses

### C8 — Moesgaard et al. (2022), periodization meta-analysis

https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01636-1

Abstract reviewed. Volume-equated periodized training favored 1RM strength; comparable hypertrophy differences were not detected. Undulation benefits for strength were more evident in trained than untrained participants.

Implication: the Foundation/Evolution architecture is a useful organizational choice, not proof that changing phases or exercises at week five causes greater hypertrophy. Strength programming deserves separate consideration of practice and loading patterns.

### C9 — Deload trials

- Coleman et al. (2024): https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.16777 . Abstract reviewed. A one-week complete training cessation midway through nine weeks did not enhance measured lower-body hypertrophy; continuous training produced greater strength gains in that trial.
- “Effects of deload periods...” (2026): https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-40612-5 . Abstract reviewed. Reduced volume/frequency in weeks four/eight of a small novice unilateral trial produced no detected between-condition difference in muscle thickness or strength-endurance. This differs from complete cessation and from advanced whole-body training.

Implication: do not label every pause a scientifically optimized deload or promise a rebound in growth. Planned reduced-work periods and unplanned absence need distinct meanings. Preserve real workout records and resume sensibly; do not mark missed sessions complete or cram all missed work into a short recovery window.

## Update 7 — Additional bodybuilding approaches and source-quality checks

### B10 — Mentzer historical analysis

https://www.muscleandfitness.com/flexonline/training/mike-mentzers-high-intensity-workout/

Article by Greg Merritt reviewed. It distinguishes Mentzer's earlier competitive approach from his later increasingly low-volume recommendations and describes partner-assisted beyond-failure methods. This is historical secondary analysis with attributed quotations, not a full original Mentzer book or a controlled test.

Implication: “Mentzer training” itself changed over time. Forced reps, negatives and rest-pause are not interchangeable with ordinary straight sets and should not become default unsupervised novice work.

### B11 — Frank Zane, quick leg/torso routines

https://frankzane.com/blog-52-quick-leg-torso-routines/

Official-site article reviewed. Describes short, dense tri-set routines with exercise-specific work and states that the discussed season's goal was maintaining bodyweight. Its reported set totals use its own presentation of tri-sets; do not equate those totals automatically with fully rested straight sets.

Implication: a time-efficient personal maintenance routine does not authenticate an optimal hypertrophy prescription for every user. Dense sessions require different timing assumptions from conventional straight-set training.

### B12 — Jay Cutler's reported back session

https://barbend.com/news/jay-cutler-guide-to-a-massive-back/

Secondary report of a February 2024 video; article text reviewed. Includes multiple complementary back exercises, usually three working sets each, and separates two initial warm-up sets.

Arithmetic check: the headline says twenty-one working sets, but its listed table contains six exercises with three working sets each plus one deadlift set: nineteen working sets. Adding the two listed warm-ups gives twenty-one total sets. Do not propagate the headline as a verified working-set total.

Implication: three sets per exercise can be part of a substantial professional session, and accurate reporting requires an explicit distinction between warm-ups, exercises, sets and total weekly muscle work.

### B13 — Phil Heath shoulder coaching report

https://barbend.com/news/phil-heath-teaches-will-tennyson-how-to-train-shoulders/

Secondary report of a September 2023 session reviewed. Starts with rear-delt work to address the trainee's relative weakness, then includes lateral/front raises and presses. The specific order reflects a stated physique priority. The original video was not accessed.

Implication: priority-based order and complementary shoulder work are more meaningful than a single undifferentiated “shoulder” slot. Do not assume every user needs this exact amount of front-delt work in addition to pressing.

### B14 — Lee Haney back routine report

## Update 8 — Specific muscle coverage, beyond generic movement slots

### C10 — Seated versus prone leg curls

Maeo et al. (2021), https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000002523 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7969179/

Abstract plus relevant full-text participant, limitation and conclusion sections reviewed. Twenty young adults without systematic resistance training in the prior year trained each leg in a different position. Seated curls produced greater whole-hamstring growth, particularly in the biarticular muscles; both positions provided protection against later exercise-induced damage. The authors warn against assuming every other exercise/muscle behaves identically.

Implication: posture and muscle length can change the stimulus even within a movement family. A generic “posterior” category is insufficient to establish comprehensive hamstring programming.

### C11 — Standing versus seated calf raises

Maeo et al. (2023), https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2023.1272106 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10753835/

Abstract plus relevant full-text participants and conclusion reviewed. Fourteen untrained adults used a within-person design. Standing training produced substantially greater gastrocnemius growth; soleus changes did not meaningfully differ between positions.

Implication: calf coverage should distinguish the muscles and knee position. The common simplification that seated work is necessarily better for soleus growth is not established by this comparison. Nor does the study show that every program must contain both variations.

### C12 — Overhead versus neutral-arm cable triceps extension

Maeo et al. (2023 publication), https://doi.org/10.1080/17461391.2022.2100279

Abstract reviewed. Twenty-one adults trained the arms in different shoulder positions. Overhead training produced greater triceps hypertrophy despite lower absolute loads. This is an exercise-specific trial, not a universal rule that lighter weights or more stretching always produce greater growth.

Implication: absolute weight is an inadequate measure of exercise quality. Complementary shoulder positions can matter when evaluating direct triceps coverage.

### C13 — Squat versus leg extension

Kassiano et al. (2026), https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000005338

Abstract reviewed. Sixty-three untrained young women performed Smith-machine squats or leg extensions. Regional growth differed: leg extensions favored rectus femoris outcomes, while squats favored a distal vastus lateralis measure. Strength gains were task-specific.

Implication: a squat slot does not establish uniform development of every quadriceps region. A bodybuilding plan may reasonably combine movement types, without requiring every user to perform one specific machine exercise.

### C14 — Hip thrust versus squat

Plotkin et al. (2023), https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2023.1279170 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10593473/

Abstract plus relevant full-text limitations/conclusion reviewed. Similar measured gluteal growth, more thigh growth with squats, and strength gains favoring the trained tasks. Little hamstring growth was observed in either group. Acute EMG did not consistently predict later growth. Participants were young and untrained; the authors limit generalization accordingly.

Implication: do not count all hip-extension exercises as equivalent hamstring work. Do not rank exercises for hypertrophy solely by acute EMG or perceived activation. This trial cannot quantify the hamstring stimulus of every bridge or hinge variation.

## Update 9 — Effort estimation needs teaching

### C15 — RIR trainability

https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-026-01997-y ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13499321/

Abstract plus relevant full-text participants and conclusion reviewed. Twenty-six recreationally active adults with prior training but no RIR/RPE regulation experience completed six bench/leg press sessions. Estimation accuracy improved. The study was powered for within-person session changes, not definitive equivalence between age groups.

Implication: entering an RIR number is not proof that it is accurate. Teach and revisit the concept. Supervised research procedures that continue sets to failure do not automatically justify identical unsupervised calibration for every exercise/user.

### C16 — “Without Fail” single-set trial (2025)

https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000003728

Abstract reviewed. Forty-two resistance-trained men/women performed one set of nine exercises twice weekly, either to failure or with two RIR. Both groups improved; several hypertrophy outcomes modestly favored failure. No higher-volume group was included.

Implication: low-volume programs can produce gains; this does not establish that they maximize gains compared with a well-designed higher-volume alternative. Conversely, it directly cautions against declaring a routine ineffective just because the number of sets looks small.
